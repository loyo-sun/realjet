## Why headcount should be estimated before the equipment list

Buyers often ask how many workers a precast beam production line needs. A supplier cannot answer responsibly from daily output alone. The same target can involve different products, mould occupancy, shift patterns, lifting methods, reinforcement work and quality controls. Automation may remove one repetitive task while creating new needs for setup, inspection or technical support.

The useful purchasing question is narrower: which tasks must be covered on each shift, how many positions must work at the same time, and which skills are available locally? Answer that before comparing line concepts. The result becomes a staffing basis for the [precast beam production line proposal](/marketing/precast-beam-factory/en/), not a promise that one standard crew fits every factory.

## Build the estimate from the production sequence

Start with one production cycle for each beam family. List the operations from mould preparation through reinforcement, prestressing where applicable, concrete placement, curing, release, demoulding, transfer and final inspection. Add cleaning, changeover, material replenishment and documentation. These supporting tasks consume real shift time even when they do not appear in the headline cycle.

For every task, record five inputs:

1. planned frequency per shift;
2. expected duration under the proposed method;
3. number of people who must work at the same time;
4. skill or authorisation required;
5. equipment or shared resource needed.

Do not total the durations and divide by shift hours yet. First identify concurrency. A crane operator may support several stations, but cannot lift two items at once. The same quality technician may cover several inspections if their timing does not overlap. A two-person task still needs two people present even if it lasts only a short time.

## Distinguish direct labour from shift coverage

Direct task hours show workload. Shift coverage shows the people who must be available when production is running. Both belong in the purchasing model.

The task-hour calculation can begin with:

`task hours per shift = frequency x duration x people per occurrence`

Use observed times from an existing plant when possible. For a new operation, ask suppliers to state their assumptions and the boundary of each estimate. A quotation that says "six operators" should also say which work is included, which shift pattern applies and which tasks are shared.

Then add roles that may not track each beam directly. Examples include shift supervision, quality release, maintenance response, batching support, stores or logistics, and production control. Some roles may serve more than one line. Others may need dedicated coverage because local rules, equipment risk or the buyer's quality plan require it.

Leave relief, breaks, absence and training coverage visible rather than hiding them inside an unexplained percentage. The buyer should apply its own employment rules and calendar.

## Compare automation by the tasks it changes

Automation labels do not show labour impact. Ask each supplier to mark the exact production task that a proposed function changes.

| Proposed function | Buyer-side staffing question |
| --- | --- |
| Hydraulic mould operation | Who performs setup, confirms closure and handles abnormal conditions? |
| Mould or component transfer | Who controls the move, clears the route and coordinates lifting interfaces? |
| Concrete distribution | Which placing, guiding, cleaning and replenishment tasks remain manual? |
| Automated curing control | Who loads recipes, checks sensors and authorises release? |
| Production management system | Who enters plans, maintains master data and responds to alarms? |

The [NIST Manufacturing Extension Partnership's Automation 101 guidance](https://www.nist.gov/mep/manufacturing-reports/best-practices) recommends setting automation goals, selecting high-impact projects and preparing employees for new technology. Applied to a line purchase, that means linking every automation item to a named production problem and a training requirement. A lower operator count on a sales slide has little value if the buyer cannot identify the work that disappeared or the skills that replaced it.

## Map roles and skills behind each job title

Prepare a role matrix for the proposed line. It can include production operators, reinforcement and prestressing personnel, concrete placing personnel, crane or transfer operators, quality inspectors, maintenance technicians, control-system support and shift leadership. The exact labels depend on local practice.

For each role, record:

- tasks and equipment covered;
- shift and on-call coverage;
- required licence, trade or internal authorisation;
- training supplied with the line;
- training the buyer must arrange locally;
- language and documentation needs;
- backup coverage during absence or breakdown.

NIST describes workforce planning as an assessment of goals, challenges and gaps, followed by recruitment and training actions. Its [manufacturing workforce development guidance](https://www.nist.gov/mep/manufacturing-workforce-development) is general rather than precast-specific, but the sequence is useful: identify the work, compare it with available capability, then plan how to close the gaps.

## Put staffing assumptions into the RFQ

Suppliers need the same staffing brief if their proposals are to be comparable. Include:

- beam types, dimensions and production quantities;
- working days, shifts and effective hours per shift;
- target output and planned ramp-up stages;
- proposed production method and mould strategy;
- reinforcement, prestressing, concrete and curing assumptions;
- available lifting and material-handling resources;
- tasks that the buyer expects to automate;
- local skill availability and restricted occupations;
- maintenance and technical-support model;
- buyer and supplier responsibilities for training.

Ask for a station-by-station staffing table at normal production and during changeover. The supplier should identify any peak that requires temporary extra personnel. It should also state whether batching, reinforcement fabrication, laboratory work, yard handling and dispatch are inside or outside the estimate.

## Test the proposal against three operating conditions

One steady-state number is too narrow for a purchasing decision. Review the crew under three conditions.

The first is start-up. New operators need supervision, training and slower task times. The second is normal production, once the planned methods are stable. The third is a credible disruption, such as a product change, shared-crane conflict or unavailable specialist. The purpose is not to predict every event. It is to see whether the proposed line relies on one person or resource with no practical backup.

Also examine how the estimate changes if the buyer adds a second shift. Some support roles can be shared across the day. Others must be duplicated. Maintenance response, quality release and supervision often need an explicit coverage decision rather than an assumption.

## Use the staffing model to compare total purchase risk

Convert the agreed role matrix into local labour cost, recruitment lead time and training effort. Keep those buyer-side costs separate from the equipment quotation. A more automated proposal may reduce direct task hours but need stronger electrical, controls or maintenance support. A simpler line may require more repetitive manual work but fit the available workforce better.
