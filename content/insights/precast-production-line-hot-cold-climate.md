## Climate is an equipment input

Two buyers can order the same beam section, the same moulds and the same output, and end up with different plants because one builds in a coastal climate at 30 °C and the other at 1,800 m where the night temperature falls below freezing for four months. The concrete is the same. The equipment that keeps it workable, curable and saleable is not.

Most line enquiries carry the annual output, the component range and the factory area. Far fewer carry the climate data that decides whether the plant needs an insulated enclosure, a steam supply, chilled water, or all three. That gap usually surfaces during commissioning, when the changes are expensive.

The effects show up in curing, in batching, and in the number of moulds a plant owns for the same daily output.

## What heat does to fresh concrete

High ambient temperature accelerates everything. Cement hydrates faster, slump is lost sooner, and the window between mixing and finishing narrows. Water evaporates more quickly from an exposed surface, which raises the risk of plastic shrinkage cracking before the concrete has any strength to resist it.

The American Concrete Institute covers this in ACI 305R, Guide to Hot Weather Concreting. ACI 301 and ACI 305.1 limit the concrete temperature at the time of placement to 95 °F, which is 35 °C. The [FHWA review of curing practice](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/infrastructure/pavements/pccp/05038/002.cfm) notes that ACI 305R itself sets no maximum placing temperature, so the limit a plant works to comes from the specification being followed, not from the guide alone.

Batching may need chilled water or ice. Aggregate stockpiles may need shading or sprinkling. The time between discharge and casting becomes a scheduling constraint, which affects how many casting beds can be served by one concrete distributor. And curing has to start earlier, because the surface dries faster.

## What cold does to strength gain

Below about 5 °C the hydration reaction slows sharply, and at freezing point it effectively stops. Concrete that freezes before it has developed useful strength can be permanently damaged, and no amount of later curing repairs it.

ACI 306R, Guide to Cold Weather Concreting, gives the working rules. Insulation is used to hold a minimum concrete temperature of 10 °C when ambient is below that, and a single freezing event has to be avoided until the concrete has reached a strength of 3.5 MPa. The same FHWA review sets out how those thresholds sit alongside other guidance, and notes that EN 206 accounts for climatic conditions during curing through qualitative categories rather than through an evaporation rate.

Cold weather adds a heat source, usually steam, hot water or an insulated enclosure. It also lengthens the curing cycle, which changes the number of moulds needed for a fixed daily output, and it makes stripping strength the controlling parameter rather than time. The plant then needs a way to measure strength rather than a clock on the wall.

## The equipment that changes

Curing is where the difference is largest. A line in a hot climate may need nothing more than covers and a water spray, with attention to wind and sun. A line in a cold climate needs a curing chamber or an insulated enclosure with a controlled heat supply, and that becomes a structural and services item rather than an accessory.

Batching follows. Hot climates push towards chilled water, aggregate shading and possibly flaked ice. Cold climates push towards heated water and sometimes aggregate heating, with the mixer and the water tank insulated.

The casting area itself may need enclosure. An open casting bed is workable in most climates for part of the year, but a plant that must produce through winter or through a monsoon season is effectively an indoor plant, and the building becomes part of the capital scope.

Moulds respond to temperature too, though less obviously. Steel expands, and a long mould set up at 5 °C is not the same length at 40 °C. Tolerances measured in a cold workshop and repeated in a hot casting hall will not agree unless the reference temperature is stated. That belongs in the mould specification, and it is easy to leave out when the enquiry is written in a temperate country and the plant is built in another.

## Enclosure, ventilation and condensation

Once a plant is enclosed, the building services become part of the line design. Ventilation has to remove heat and moisture without creating draughts across fresh concrete. In a heated hall, warm moist air meeting a cold steel roof condenses and drips onto the work, which is a finish defect and a curing problem at the same time.

The practical questions for the enquiry are unglamorous: what insulation level, what air change rate, how is condensation controlled, and who supplies and commissions the heating or cooling plant. These are usually outside the scope of a precast equipment supplier, and that is exactly why the boundary has to be written down before the contract rather than discovered at commissioning.

## Batching, water and aggregates

The batching plant is often specified separately from the casting equipment, which is where climate requirements get lost. A plant ordered for a temperate site may arrive with no provision for chilled water, no insulation on the water tank, and no capacity for the longer mixing time that a cold mix needs.

Two numbers decide most of this. The required concrete temperature at discharge, and the range of ambient temperatures the plant must operate across. With those two, a batching supplier can size the chiller or the heater. Without them, the plant is sized for a mild day and the buyer discovers the gap in the first heat wave or the first frost.

## Labour, shifts and the working day

Climate also changes the shift pattern, and the shift pattern changes the output calculation. In a hot climate, casting often moves to early morning or to night, which affects lighting, supervision and the number of hours the batching plant runs. In a cold climate, the curing cycle dominates, and the plant may run the same hours with more moulds in circulation.

A feasibility study that assumes three shifts at constant output across the year is usually wrong in both directions. The honest approach is to model the worst month, not the average one, and to size the mould count and the curing capacity against that month.

## Data to collect before the enquiry

| Item | Why the supplier needs it | Typical source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly ambient temperature range, day and night | Decides heating, cooling and enclosure | Local meteorological records |
| Design maximum and minimum for equipment rating | Sets the equipment specification, not the average | Project design basis |
| Wind speed and prevailing direction | Evaporation rate and enclosure design | Meteorological records, site survey |
| Relative humidity by season | Curing method and evaporation control | Meteorological records |
| Solar exposure of the casting area | Surface temperature and shade requirement | Site layout and orientation |
| Rainfall and season | Whether the casting area must be covered | Meteorological records |
| Altitude | Concrete temperature and equipment derating | Site survey |
| Required concrete temperature at discharge | Sizing of chillers, heaters and insulation | Mix design and specification |

## Who owns what

| Decision | Owner | Why it sits there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Climate design basis for the plant | Buyer or its engineer | Comes from the site and the project specification |
| Concrete temperature limits | Designer or specifier | Follows the governing concrete standard |
| Heating and cooling capacity | Equipment supplier, sized from the design basis | Requires the numbers above to be fixed first |
| Building enclosure and insulation | Buyer, with a separate contractor | Rarely inside the precast equipment package |
| Curing method and cycle | Supplier proposes, buyer approves | Depends on the mix and the component |
| Condensation control in the hall | Building designer | A building services problem, not a mould problem |
| Shift pattern and output guarantee | Buyer | Sets what the line must achieve in the worst month |

## Where the line design starts

The climate data costs nothing to collect and changes the equipment list materially. A buyer who brings the monthly temperature range, the wind and humidity record and the required discharge temperature to the first meeting gets a line sized for the site instead of a line sized for a brochure.
